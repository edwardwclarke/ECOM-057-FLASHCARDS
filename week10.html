What is convergence trading?
A strategy where traders profit when related asset prices move back together after diverging.
Who is the originator of convergence trading?
Nunzio Tartaglia at Morgan Stanley in the mid-1980s.
How does convergence trading stay market neutral?
By using long/short positions so gains/losses are balanced.
What is pair trading?
A convergence strategy using two related stocks with positions opened when their price spread diverges.
What is the trading rule in Gatev et al. (2006)?
Open a dollar-neutral position when spread > 2x historical SD; close when it converges.
What were Gatev et al. (2006)'s findings?
Strategy gave 1% monthly excess returns, better than S&P500, with lower volatility.
What did Jacobs & Weber (2015) analyze?
Pair trading globally, found returns not explained by standard risk factors.
What are standard risk factors?
Size, value, momentum, reversals, and liquidity.
How do macro news impact pair trading returns?
Returns are higher after macro news than idiosyncratic news.
What increases returns after macro news?
Low analyst coverage, high volatility, or high forecast disagreement.
What are arbitrage limits?
Barriers that make it harder to trade, like high VIX or wide bid-ask spreads.
When does pair trading perform best?
When divergence happens on days with high arbitrage limits.
What is cointegration?
A long-term relationship where the difference between two prices tends to return to the mean.
What is the metaphor for cointegration?
A drunk man and his dog on a leash.
What is correlation?
A short-term relationship where prices move in the same direction but can drift apart.
What is the metaphor for correlation?
Two drunk men walking in the same direction.
Where does cointegration appear in finance?
Interest rates, currencies, indexes, futures, commodities.
Can cointegration relationships break?
Yes, for example between IBM and Microsoft.
What is index tracking?
A strategy to copy the performance of a market index like the S&P 500.
What is a fully indexed fund?
It replicates the full index using value-weighted holdings.
What is a sampled index fund?
It tracks a large index using a representative sample of stocks.
Do active managers often beat the index?
No — in most years from 1971–2010, fewer than 50% did.
Why is this underperformance robust?
It holds when adjusting for risk, survivor bias, and fund styles.
Why do active funds underperform?
Due to high trading costs, taxes, and overtrading.
What are alternatives to indexing?
Index futures, ETFs, enhanced index funds.
What are enhanced index funds?
Low-cost funds that try to slightly outperform the index.
What is short-term price reversal?
Stocks that go up one month tend to fall the next and vice versa.
Why does short-term reversal happen?
Due to market overreaction to news.
What did Blitz et al. (2010) find?
Short-term reversal strategies gave strong returns from 1929–2009.
What is price momentum?
Stocks that perform well over 6–12 months tend to keep rising.
Who introduced momentum strategy?
Jegadeesh and Titman (1993, 2001).
What did Damodaran (2012) show about momentum?
Top-performing stocks gave higher excess returns over a year.
How does volume affect momentum?
Momentum is stronger in high-volume stocks.
What is upside vs downside momentum?
Upside momentum dominates over long periods; downside in short ones.
What is growth effect on momentum?
Momentum is stronger in high-growth stocks (high price-to-book).
What is long-term price reversal?
Over 5 years, past winners often become losers and vice versa.
What did Fama & French (1988) find?
Stronger long-term reversal in small-cap stocks.
What is the tipping point?
The moment when momentum fades and price reversal takes over.
Why is the tipping point important?
Momentum strategies crash if held too long.
What’s the overall pattern of prices?
Momentum in the medium term, reversal in the short and long term.
